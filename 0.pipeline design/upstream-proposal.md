# From courses to topics: a proposal for the upstream pipeline

This note proposes how to derive, from the syllabi of the current courses, the list of topics on which module generation works. It accompanies the revised schema in `analysis-pipeline.mmd` (rendered by `render.sh`), where the steps proposed here have a dashed border and the existing ones a solid border. The downstream part of the schema, from the solver inputs to human review, is unchanged.

## What module generation needs

The module engine partitions topics into modules with a constraint solver. It treats every topic as an indivisible unit and reads, for each one, its size in ECTS (European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System credits, equivalent to the Italian CFU), the topics it depends on, and its affinity with the other topics. Constraints such as "keep introductory material apart from advanced material" or "keep generic concepts apart from language-specific ones" also require topic attributes that record level and technology. The quality of a module proposal therefore depends first on the topic list. Two topics that mean the same thing split a module that should be one, a topic as broad as a whole course cannot be distributed over modules, and a wrong size estimate distorts every ECTS bound.

## The current extraction and its limits

Today a large language model (LLM) extracts topics from the 293 courses of the corpus in a single sequential pass. Each request contains the current topic list together with one course's title, learning outcomes and contents, and the model replies with changes to the list and with the topics the course covers. The latest attempt (`data/restructuring/attempt-2026-10-06-18-00`, which reuses the topics of `attempt-2026-10-06-13-26`) produced 1,861 topics, with a median of 15 per course. Half of them (928) belong to a single course.

Because the same pass both reads syllabi and maintains the global list, the list grows with the order in which courses are processed, and the model adds variants of existing topics instead of reusing them. The result contains near-duplicates such as `algorithmic_problem_solving`, `algorithms_programming_basics` and `algorithms_and_programs`, or `recursion` and `recursive_algorithms`. Granularity is also uneven. Specific topics such as `ll1_parsing` sit next to topics as broad as a course, such as `python_programming_basics` ("Introduction to programming in Python") or `computer_hardware_architecture`, which 33 courses share. The same concept often appears once per programming language, as the prefixes of many keys show (27 start with `python_`, 25 with `c_`, 13 with `java_`, 12 with `cpp_`).

Topic sizes are estimated afterwards by spreading each course's credits evenly over its topics and taking, for each topic, the median of its shares across courses (median 0.35 ECTS). This ignores how much of a course a topic actually takes. Finally, the extraction records neither the syllabus passage a topic comes from nor attributes such as level and technology, and it produces no dependencies between topics. The syllabi cannot supply dependencies directly, since none of the scraped course pages has a prerequisites section. The available evidence is the course contents (291 courses), the learning outcomes (all courses, in Italian or English), the bibliography, the teaching methods and the assessment methods.

## The proposed pipeline

We propose to separate reading the syllabi from building the topic list, and to make each step produce an inspectable artefact that later steps, and people, can correct. The table summarises the seven stages, which the following paragraphs describe in order.

| Stage | Method | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Evidence preparation | code | cleaned outcomes and numbered content items, course facts |
| 2. Per-course topic extraction | LLM, each course independently | raw topics with evidence and attributes |
| 3. Canonicalisation | embeddings and LLM | canonical topics, hierarchy, raw-to-canonical mapping |
| 4. Topic review | people | corrections recorded in the mapping |
| 5. Re-annotation | LLM, closed list of topics | course × topic coverage with weights |
| 6. Derived inputs | code | topic ECTS, attributes, affinity |
| 7. Dependency proposal and review | LLM, then people | topic dependencies with provenance |

Evidence preparation takes, for each course, the learning outcomes and the contents, in English when available and otherwise in Italian. It removes boilerplate such as instructor names and office hours, and splits the contents into numbered items that later stages can cite. It also keeps the course facts that constraints may need, namely credits, scientific sector, teaching language, campus, degree programmes, and whether the teaching methods include a laboratory.

Per-course extraction then asks the LLM to list the topics of one syllabus at a time, without showing it any global list. Courses become independent, so they can run in parallel and be cached, and the result no longer depends on their order. For each topic the model returns a name and a one-sentence description, the content item or outcome it comes from, an estimate of the share of the course it takes, its level (introductory, intermediate or advanced), its kind (a concept or technique as opposed to a specific tool or language), and the technology it refers to, if any. The prompt fixes a target size of roughly four to eight hours of lectures, which corresponds to 0.5–1 ECTS, and illustrates it with examples of topics that are too broad and too narrow.

Canonicalisation turns the raw topics into the canonical list. We embed every raw topic (name, description and evidence) with the sentence-embedding model already used for clustering, and group candidates whose descriptions are close. For each group, the LLM decides which topics are the same, which one subsumes another, and which are related but distinct. The output is the canonical list, a two-level hierarchy of parent and child topics, and a mapping from raw to canonical topics that preserves provenance. Size checks complete this stage. A topic that many courses share, that takes a large share of the courses teaching it, or whose description lists several sub-topics is split, and a topic below the target size is merged into its parent. For concepts that recur across languages we propose to store a generic topic with a technology attribute, so that `c_pointers` and `cpp_pointers` become `pointers` with technologies C and C++. The engine can then keep generic and language-specific material apart through constraints rather than through duplicated topics. Optionally, names and sizes can be anchored to the knowledge units of the ACM/IEEE-CS/AAAI Computer Science Curricula 2023 (CS2023), which also suggest teaching hours. Since service courses reach well beyond computer science (digital health, cultural heritage, fashion), such a taxonomy can only serve as an anchor and cannot replace the canonical list.

Topic review lets people correct the canonical list by merging, splitting and renaming topics, in the same way as the existing review page does for modules. Every decision is written to the mapping file. Re-running the extraction therefore reapplies the corrections instead of discarding them, and the mapping keeps a versioned record of why the list looks as it does.

Re-annotation asks the LLM, for each course, which canonical topics its syllabus covers, with what share and on what evidence. Choosing from a fixed list is considerably more consistent than open extraction, and it also catches topics that open extraction missed in some courses. To keep requests small, each course sees only the canonical topics retrieved as similar to its syllabus. The result is a course × topic matrix with weights.

The derived inputs follow from that matrix without further model calls. A topic's ECTS becomes the median of its weighted shares rather than of an even split, and the spread of those shares across courses is kept as a measure of uncertainty. The attributes from extraction, reconciled during canonicalisation, are written to the topic file. Affinity is recomputed on the canonical topics, both from co-teaching and from the description embeddings, with the consensus method already implemented in `network_analysis/topic_affinity.py`. On co-teaching alone, every course's exclusive topics always end up together, so the description layer is what allows modules to group topics across courses.

Dependency proposal asks the LLM to judge only plausible pairs, meaning topics taught together, topics with similar descriptions, or topics in the same branch of the hierarchy. The model states a direction and a justification for each pair, and the order of a course's content items serves as weak supporting evidence. After a cycle check, people review the proposed edges and mark the accepted ones as checked, so that the dependency file records the provenance of every edge.

## Quality gates and human judgement

Before the topic list reaches the solver, four measurements should show whether it is fit for module generation. The first counts the pairs of canonical topics whose descriptions remain closer than the duplicate threshold. The second is the distribution of topic sizes and of topics per course. The third is the share of content items and learning outcomes covered by at least one topic. The fourth is the agreement between two extraction runs with a different model or seed. These measurements flag problems but cannot tell whether the topics are right, so every stage that relies on a model ends with a human check. Reviewers judge the raw topics of each course against its syllabus (missing or invented topics, wrong evidence, size or level), the canonicalisation decisions (merges, splits and parent–child relations), the re-annotation of each course, and the proposed dependencies. Their judgements are stored with the artefacts of each stage, so that a re-run reapplies them, and the share of model output that reviewers accept at each stage tracks the quality of that stage over time.

We propose to start with a pilot on a small set of service courses, the primary scope of the working group, chosen so that the review effort stays manageable while the stages and their review interfaces take shape.

## Impact on the repository

The proposal reuses the infrastructure of `restructuring/`, that is structured replies with validation, retries and caching, together with the course corpus and the embeddings and affinity code in `network_analysis/`. What changes is the extraction prompt, whose replies become per course and carry evidence and attributes, the new canonicalisation, re-annotation and dependency stages, and the size estimate. The contract with the module engine remains `topics.yml`, `dependencies.yml` and `affinity.yml`. The topic file gains attribute fields, which new constraint kinds can use, and every dependency records its source.

## Open questions for the group

Some choices are not technical and need the group's agreement. The first is the target size of a topic, for which we suggest four to eight hours of lectures. The second is whether to anchor the canonical list to CS2023 or to keep it fully data-driven. The third is the policy for language-specific material, where we suggest generic topics with a technology attribute. We also need to decide who reviews the topic list and the dependencies, and how much of the corpus to cover. Extracting topics for all courses keeps the internal DISI teaching as context for affinity, while module generation can focus on the service courses.
